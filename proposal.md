# AI and the Future of Work: Policy Proposal

## To:
Deputy Secretary, Employment & Skills

## Executive Summary
Artificial intelligence (AI) is transforming labour markets by reshaping job tasks, altering skill demand, and influencing wage distribution. While AI has the potential to increase productivity and economic growth, it also risks displacing workers and increasing inequality in the short term. According to the World Economic Forum (2023), AI and automation technologies are expected to significantly reshape global labour markets over the next decade, increasing both productivity opportunities and displacement risks. This proposal evaluates three policy responses: reskilling programs, wage insurance schemes, and competition and data policy reform. 

It recommends a combined policy package that supports worker transition, promotes inclusive growth, and ensures competitive market structures. Implementation should occur over a 3–5 year period with clear performance indicators including employment transitions, wage recovery, and training participation rates. The proposal focuses specifically on balancing economic efficiency with equity outcomes during labour market transition. This approach ensures that AI adoption delivers long-term economic benefits while minimising social disruption.

## Problem Definition & Context
AI is not simply replacing jobs, but transforming the tasks that make up jobs. Advances in machine learning and automation technologies allow firms to substitute routine and predictable tasks while enhancing productivity in non-routine work. Firms increasingly adopt AI technologies because they can reduce labour costs, improve efficiency, and increase productivity. Similar to previous technological revolutions such as industrial automation and the internet, AI functions as a general-purpose technology capable of transforming multiple industries simultaneously.

The economic impact of AI operates through three key channels:

- **Automation effect**: AI replaces routine labour  
- **Augmentation effect**: AI enhances worker productivity  
- **Creation effect**: AI generates new jobs and industries

The World Economic Forum (2023) estimated that 44% of workers’ skills are expected to change within five years due to AI and automation technologies. This highlights the scale of labour market adjustment likely to occur across advanced economies.  

The main policy challenge is the transition gap. Workers displaced from declining roles often lack the skills required for emerging jobs, leading to unemployment, underemployment, and wage pressure. In Australia, occupations involving routine administrative and clerical tasks are considered particularly exposed to AI-driven automation. This creates significant policy challenges for governments attempting to support labour market transition while maintaining economic growth.
## Analysis of Impacts
### Employment and Tasks
AI is expected to reduce demand for routine and middle-skill jobs while increasing demand for high-skill and service-based roles. This contributes to labour market polarisation, where middle-skill routine occupations decline while demand increases in both high-skill analytical roles and lower-paid service occupations.

Economists such as David Autor argue that AI primarily affects tasks rather than entire occupations. While some repetitive functions may be automated, many jobs are likely to evolve instead of disappear completely. For example, accountants may increasingly rely on AI for data processing while still performing judgement-based advisory work.

The World Economic Forum (2023) estimated that AI and automation technologies could displace approximately 83 million jobs globally by 2027 while also creating 69 million new roles. This suggests that although AI may create employment opportunities, the transition process is likely to be uneven and disruptive.

### Wages and Inequality
AI increases the marginal productivity of workers who possess complementary digital and analytical skills. As a result, these workers are likely to experience higher wages and stronger employment opportunities.

In contrast, workers performing routine or predictable tasks may experience downward wage pressure as firms substitute labour with automation technologies. Without government intervention, these trends may widen income inequality and reduce social mobility, particularly among younger and lower-skilled workers.

According to IMF research, approximately 40% of global employment is exposed to AI technologies, with advanced economies facing both the greatest productivity benefits and highest displacement risks. In contrast, displaced workers may face wage declines, increasing income inequality.

### Industrial Structure
Firms with access to large datasets and advanced AI capabilities gain a competitive advantage, increasing market concentration. This raises concerns about reduced competition and growing monopoly power within digital markets. AI development relies heavily on access to large datasets, advanced computational infrastructure, and substantial capital investment. These barriers to entry advantage large technology firms and may reduce opportunities for smaller competitors. Increased market concentration may also allow dominant firms to capture a disproportionate share of productivity gains, further contributing to inequality across industries and labour markets.

### Distributional Effects
The impact of AI is uneven:

Lower-skilled workers face significantly higher displacement risks because their occupations often involve routine and predictable tasks that are easier to automate. In contrast, workers with university qualifications and advanced digital skills are more likely to benefit from AI augmentation.

The impact of AI is also geographically uneven. Urban regions are more likely to attract AI-related investment due to stronger digital infrastructure and higher concentrations of skilled labour. Regional communities dependent on routine occupations may therefore experience slower employment growth and weaker economic adjustment.

Knowledge-intensive industries such as finance, technology, and professional services are expected to benefit most from AI adoption, while industries heavily reliant on repetitive administrative work face greater disruption.

These effects create both economic opportunities and equity challenges.
## Policy Options
| Policy Option | Effectiveness | Equity | Cost | Feasibility |
|--------------|-------------|--------|------|-------------|
| Reskilling Programs | High | High | High | Medium |
| Wage Insurance | Medium | High | Medium | High |
| Competition Policy | Medium | Medium | Low | Medium |

### 1. Reskilling Programs
Government-funded training programs aimed at developing skills complementary to AI. 

**Pros:**
- Addresses skill mismatch  
- Supports long-term employment

Reskilling programs improve long-term labour market adaptability by helping workers transition into industries where AI complements rather than replaces labour. However, these programs require substantial government funding and may produce weak outcomes if training is not aligned with industry demand.

**Cons:**
- High cost  
- Uncertain outcomes  

### 2. Wage Insurance
Provides temporary income support for displaced workers.

**Pros:**
- Reduces financial stress  
- Encourages job transitions  

Wage insurance can stabilise household incomes during periods of economic disruption and reduce financial stress for displaced workers. However, long-term reliance on income support may reduce incentives for retraining if policies are poorly designed.

**Cons:**
- Does not directly improve skills  

### 3. Competition & Data Policy
Ensures fair access to data and limits market concentration.

**Pros:**
- Promotes innovation  
- Prevents monopoly power  

Competition and data regulation can encourage broader innovation and prevent excessive market concentration among dominant AI firms. However, excessive regulation may reduce private investment incentives and slow technological development.

**Cons:**
- Complex to implement  
## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan
A combined policy approach is recommended:

A combined policy package is recommended because no single policy can fully address the labour market effects of AI. Reskilling improves long-term workforce adaptability, wage insurance protects workers during transition periods, and competition policy helps ensure productivity gains are distributed more broadly across the economy.

- Targeted reskilling programs  
- Wage insurance schemes  
- Pro-competition data regulation  

### Implementation Timeline
- Year 1: Pilot training programs and introduce wage support  
- Years 2–3: Expand programs nationally  
- Years 4–5: Evaluate and adjust policies  

### Responsibilities
- Department of Employment & Skills  
- Treasury  
- Competition regulators  

Training initiatives could be delivered through Australia’s TAFE system in partnership with Jobs and Skills Australia and private industry stakeholders. Collaboration between government and industry is essential to ensure training programs reflect evolving labour market demands.

### Key Performance Indicators
- Employment transition rates  
- Wage recovery levels  
- Training completion rates

Although these programs involve substantial upfront fiscal costs, long-term productivity growth and reduced unemployment are likely to offset part of the financial burden over time.
  
## Risks & Mitigations
| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|-----------|
| Low training uptake | Provide incentives |
| Budget constraints | Phase implementation |
| Skill mismatch | Partner with industry |

Policy implementation may face political resistance due to budget pressures and uncertainty surrounding the pace of AI adoption. Ongoing evaluation, industry consultation, and policy flexibility will therefore be essential to ensure government responses remain effective as labour market conditions evolve.
